ABSTRACT: Covalent adaptable networks (CANs) unite recyclability and mechanical
stability by leveraging externally triggered bond exchange. However, a significant challenge
remains in achieving a sharp reversible transition between dynamic and static network
states, which broadly limits their applicability. This work introduces a merocyanine
photoacid for precise and reversible spatiotemporal control over dynamic bond exchange in
thiol−ene photopolymers. When exposed to visible light, the photoacid’s activated
spiropyran form enables dynamic network rearrangements through acid-catalyzed
transesterification. Switching off the light yields the deactivated merocyanine form and a
creep-resistant polymer network. Stress relaxation experiments clearly demonstrate a
remarkable difference in mechanical properties resulting from the photoacid’s isomerization
state. The rapid isomerization kinetics and fatigue resistance of this system are utilized to
generate gradients of active photoacid at the micrometer level. Applied in a novel mold-free
reshaping approach, bending radii are predictable based on the introduction of an empirical
model. Finally, the successful fabrication of diverse microstructures via multiphoton laser writing highlights the future potential of
these reversibly switchable CANs in light-controlled micromechanics.
1. INTRODUCTION
Thermosetting polymers, with their covalently cross-linked
network structure, offer outstanding mechanical performance
and chemical resistance. However, this robust nature comes at
a cost, as they are neither recyclable, weldable, nor malleable.
1,2
Covalent adaptable networks (CANs) aim to bridge this gap
between performance and sustainability by the introduction of
dynamic covalent bonds capable of reorganizing the network
topology in response to an external stimulus (most often
heat).
2−4
Among various dynamic bond chemistries, including
transamination of vinylogous urethanes,
5
siloxane exchange,
6
thiol−thioester exchange,
7,8
and transcarbamoylation,
9,10
trans-
esterification is a widely applied mechanism.
3,11
Required
reactive groups, i.e., ester links and hydroxy groups, are easily
introduced in various polymer systems and several catalysts
like acids, bases, and organometallic salts, can be used.
3,11−14
However, conventional, permanently active catalysts limit the
adjustment of dynamic bond exchange rates solely to the
temperature. This can lead to significant drawbacks in terms of
creep resistance, especially in applications requiring elevated
temperatures or in CANs exhibiting low glass transition
temperatures.
4
While latent acid and base catalysts provide a well-
established concept for temperature-decoupled and spatially
resolved control over dynamic bond exchange, their irrever-
sible activation pathways are a key limitation. Once activated,
they are effectively “downgraded” to conventional, perma-
nently active CANs.
7,15−19
The development of CANs aiming
for a reversible activation of covalent bond exchange is
significantly hampered by a limited number of suitable
switchable catalysts. Additional constraints include poor
solubility in the polymer, residual catalytic activity in the
deactivated state, slow switching kinetics, and poor fatigue
resistance over multiple switching cycles. Consequently, only a
small number of reversibly switchable CANs have been
developed to date.
8,10,20,21
Recently, our group demonstrated the use of a photo-
switchable nitrogen superbase, i.e., a photochromic dithieny-
lethene derivative equipped with a guanidine unit,
22
for local
and reversible control over the base-catalyzed dynamic
exchange of thioester links and thiol groups. Exposure to
visible light generated the active ring-opened isomer, while
exposure to UV light regenerated the less active ring-closed
isomer. Stress relaxation and UV−vis experiments, carried out
over multiple cycles, revealed a significant difference in
mechanical properties depending on the isomerization state,
good fatigue resistance, and fast switching kinetics in both

In a separate development, Sardon and co-workers used
different merocyanine (MC) photoacids as reversibly switch-
able catalysts. Exposing them to 440 nm LED light generated

was reobtained in the dark. When incorporated into polyur-

the stress relaxation rate based on the isomerization state of the
catalyst was achieved, pronounced relaxation was also observed
in the presence of the less active MC form. Furthermore,
switching from the MC to the SP form required a considerable
amount of time (7 days in the dark), likely due to the MC
photoacids’ limited solubility in the polymer, as dissolution
during production required significant quantities of tetrahy-
drofuran.

optimized structural properties for application in polymer
systems exhibiting a polar chain structure, as valid for most of
the CANs commonly used.
5−7,9,11

ene photopolymer, we achieve fast switching kinetics (on the

by a significant difference in pK

associative exchange between ester links and hydroxy groups.
The rapid switching kinetics is further exploited for on-demand
generation of catalyst gradients at the microscale level,
enabling relaxation-controlled reshaping of the material.
Curvatures are precisely predictable through the introduced
mathematical model.
2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
2.1. Design of a Fast Switching CAN. Transferring
efficient reversible (photo)isomerization from solution to a
solid polymer matrix is challenging. Key factors influencing the
isomerization rate are (i) concentration and solubility of the
(photo)switchable molecule, (ii) the polymer matrix’s glass
transition temperature, (iii) the free volume surrounding the
switching unit, and (iv) steric constraints of the switching
process.
23
Considering these factors, we selected an MC
photoacid (Figure 1a) with superior solubility and switching
properties in polar media, synthesized in three steps according
to the literature. Irradiation with 440 nm light yields the more
acidic SP form (pK
a
hv
= 4.03 ± 0.03), while the MC form
(pK
a
dark
= 7.33 ± 0.01) is recovered in the dark.
24
When incorporated into a covalently cross-linked polymer
with sufficient ester and hydroxy groups, transesterification-
based bond exchange proceeds rapidly in the presence of the
light-generated SP form. In the dark (MC form), dynamic
bond exchange is suppressed (Figure 1b). The progression of
acid-catalyzed transesterification within a polymer matrix has
been confirmed by various studies using different acid
catalysts.
3,14,17,19

thiol−ene photopolymers, which were obtained via air and

2.2. Switching of the Photoacid. To analyze the
efficiency, kinetics, and reversibility of isomerization between
the MC form and the SP form within the polymer network, a
50 μm thick polymer film of MF1 containing the photoacid at
a concentration of 0.75 mol % (relative to ester links) was
prepared. This lowered concentration of photoacid enabled us
to monitor the isomerization process by UV−vis spectroscopy
while preventing full absorption across the entire wavelength
range analyzed (650 to 250 nm). For quantitative switching to
the more acidic SP form, less than 480 mJ cm
−2
of 440 nm
LED light were needed (Figure 2a). The reverse reaction to
the MC form, occurring in the dark, required approximately 16
h at room temperature (Figure 2b). Interestingly, the reaction
rate showed a strong temperature dependence and could be
significantly increased by increasing the temperature (Figure
2c). For example, only 10 min was needed for complete
isomerization to the MC form at 80 °C, enabling precise
control over the reaction.
In the next step, six full switching cycles (turning the light
ON and OFF) were followed by UV−vis spectroscopy (Figure
2d). The absorption characteristics of the MC form and the SP
form remained unaffected over the entire experiment, which
highlights the outstanding fatigue resistance of the isomer-
ization process within the thiol−ene photopolymer containing
ester links and hydroxy groups for dynamic transesterification.

Second, measurements were performed under constant
exposure conditions. In accordance with the previous findings,
MF1 (Figure 3b) and MF2 (Figure 3c) samples showed only
limited relaxation in the presence of the MC form, i.e., in the
dark. Conversely, under continuous exposure to 440 nm LED
light, which generated the catalytically active SP form, a
distinct stress relaxation was observed. Furthermore, the
relaxation levels achieved at the end of these continuous
irradiation measurements largely matched the final values of
the stress relaxation experiments conducted under ON/OFF
switching of the MC photoacid (Figure 3a). To facilitate the
comparison between ON/OFF and continuous irradiation, the
respective ON/OFF curves were included in Figure 3b,c in
gray color. The measurement time under continuous
irradiation was adjusted to the cumulated segment measure-
ment time under light exposure (total measurement duration
of all five ON segments of 840 min). Additionally, for MF1
and MF2, the five respective ON segments were arranged
directly after each other (OFF segments were removed) and
compared with the measurement curves obtained under
continuous irradiation (Figure S8). The nearly identical
curve progression in both cases confirms the superior creep
resistance in the presence of the deactivated MC form and the
absence of significant side reactions.
Compared to conventional CANs, which usually contain a
permanently active catalyst and whose rate of bond exchange
increases with rising temperature,
3
thermal influences on the
introduced system are considerably more complex to describe.
This is the case because the MC photoacid’s isomerization
equilibrium is also highly temperature-dependent (Figure 2c).
Consequently, an increase in temperature does not inherently
correspond to an acceleration of stress relaxation nor does a
decrease in temperature inherently imply its deceleration. At
90 and 100 °C, an almost identical stress relaxation behavior
was observed for MF2 photopolymer samples (Figure S9).
Due to the slower reverse isomerization to the deactivated MC
form at 90 °C, a higher concentration of active catalyst, i.e., SP
form, was generated within the sample under continuous
exposure to 440 nm LED light of the same intensity. This
increased concentration of the active transesterification catalyst
counterbalanced the usually anticipated deceleration of stress
relaxation at lower temperatures. On the other hand, when the
temperature was increased to 110 °C, significantly slower stress
relaxation was observed, which is a very unique behavior in the
field of CANs. In this case, the faster reverse isomerization to
the deactivated MC form led to a reduced concentration of the
active SP form within the polymer, surpassing the conven-
tionally temperature-induced acceleration of stress relaxation
conventionally anticipated for CANs.
2.4. Gradient-Driven Reshaping. The rapid isomer-
ization of the MC form at elevated temperatures (Figure 2c)
limits the penetration depth of the 440 nm LED light. Instead
of uniform isomerization, a gradient of the catalytically active
SP form is generated along the sample’s cross-section (Figure
4a). During stress relaxation experiments, this catalyst gradient
also defines the rate of covalent bond exchange, i.e., acid-
catalyzed dynamic transesterification, across the sample.
Because of the significantly faster relaxation process on the
side facing the light compared to that on the side facing away
from the light, the sample undergoes defined bending once
residual tension is removed. This work represents a
considerable improvement over previous light-triggered
bending concepts introduced by Bowman and co-workers,
29
offering enhanced control and understanding of the shaping
process. First, quantification of the resulting bending
deformation as a function of sample thickness and relaxation
time allowed us to develop an empirical model for the accurate
prediction of bending radii. Second, the reversibility of the
bending process was demonstrated over multiple cycles; and
third, we created specialized, nonuniformly bent shapes by
adjusting the exposure pattern.
Contrary to sample thickness and relaxation time, all other
parameters, including the monomer system (MF2), measure-
ment temperature (100 °C), and set lamp intensity (69 mW
cm
−2
), were kept constant. To determine the light penetration
depth, i.e., the extension of the catalyst gradient, at the
measurement temperature, samples of different thicknesses
were placed between the 440 nm LED lamp and the sensor of
a microspectrometer. The test arrangement used is shown in
Figure S7b. While the 70 μm sample absorbed the entire base
intensity of 69.0 mW cm
−2
, a residual intensity of 0.5 mW
cm
−2
(equivalent to 0.72% of the base intensity) was detected
at the backside of the 50 μm sample (Figure S7a). Thus, the
penetration depth of the 440 nm LED light was determined to
be minimally above 50 μm. Since the empirical modeling
carried out in this section is subject to the boundary condition
that the penetration depth must be lower than the sample
thickness, only samples 70 and 100 μm thick were used for
controlled catalyst gradient-driven reshaping experiments.
Compared with the 50 μm thick MF2 sample, a reduction in
the stress relaxation rate was observed with increasing sample
thickness (Figure 4b). This was the case because an
increasingly smaller proportion of the cross-section partici-
pated in the relaxation process as a consequence of the limited
penetration depth. Accordingly, decreasing bending radii were

2.4.2. Reversibility and Advanced Geometries. Building on
the precise control and understanding of the gradient-driven
bending behavior of our photoswitchable CAN, its reversibility
was examined in a four-step experiment (Figure 5a). Switching
between the straight initial shape and a curved shape was
carried out by turning the sample (the side facing away from
the light became the side facing the light) after each stress
relaxation step. The distinct and uniform shape changes
observed were confirmed by the recorded stress relaxation
curves (Figure S13), which all show comparable rates,
indicating the absence of significant degradation phenomena.
Dynamic mechanical analysis, carried out prior to and after a
stress relaxation experiment, further verified that no significant
degradation of the polymer network occurs during light-
controlled network rearrangement (Figure S14).
Based on the same conceptional approach, different
geometries were created by adjusting the exposure pattern
(Figure 5b−d): (b) by narrowing the exposure area, a pointed
roof shape was created instead of a round arch; (c) by splitting
the exposure area, two bends separated by a flat intermediate
area were formed; and (d) by the centered exposure of the
sample’s upper half, a three-dimensional elevation was created.
Altogether, we demonstrated the mold-free creation of
advanced shapes based on the introduction of a gradient-
driven reshaping concept. Resulting curvatures were precisely
predictable as a function of sample thickness and relaxation
time, and the approach’s reversibility was verified over multiple
steps.

exploit the catalyst gradients generated at the micrometer level,
we designed the MFs to be suitable for high-precision additive
manufacturing. Multiphoton direct laser writing with a 780 nm
femtosecond laser was used for 3D printing of MF2 on
silanized glass.

Overall, our material opens novel research avenues in
microscopic engineering and testing concepts. As opposed to

Thus, tailored microscale postproduction modifications of
different geometries and their transformation into new shapes
are potentially enabled.
3. CONCLUSION
The introduction of an MC photoacid in cross-linked thiol−
ene photopolymers containing ester links and hydroxy groups
enabled spatiotemporal control over dynamic network
rearrangements. UV−vis spectroscopy confirmed efficient
isomerization to the photoacid’s active SP form (pK
a
hv
=
4.03 ± 0.03) upon exposure to 440 nm LED light. The back
reaction to the MC form (pK
a
dark
= 7.33 ± 0.01), occurring in
the dark, showed a strong temperature dependence. Un-
changed absorption behavior over six full switching cycles
indicates an outstanding fatigue resistance of the switching
process within the polymer matrix.
Stress relaxation experiments revealed a remarkable differ-
ence in mechanical properties, i.e., an ON/OFF behavior of
dynamic bond exchange, depending on the photoacid’s
isomerization state. While pronounced stress relaxation was
observed in the presence of the light-generated, active SP form,
rapid recovery of the MC form, which resulted in a creep-
