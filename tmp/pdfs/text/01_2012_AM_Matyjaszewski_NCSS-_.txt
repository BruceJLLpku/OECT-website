 Self-Healing of Covalently Cross-Linked Polymers 
by Reshufﬂ ing Thiuram Disulﬁ de Moieties 
in Air under Visible Light  
  Dr. Y . Amamoto, Prof. K. Matyjaszewski
Department of Chemistry
Carnegie Mellon University
4400 Fifth Avenue, Pittsburgh, PA 15213, USA
 E-mail:  km3b@andrew.cmu.edu

Kyushu University

 Development of environmentally friendly materials such as 
biodegradable materials [  1–3  ]  and polymers from renewable 
resources receives an increasing attention. [  4  ,  5  ]  An interesting 
approach, inspired by nature, is to prepare materials that can 
inherently repair internal and external damages. Over the past 
decade, self-healing polymeric materials were developed that 
can mend themselves and recover properties after damage. 
[  6–10  ]  
The initial example of self-healing polymers employed encap-
sulation of a monomer that was released after a crack reached 
the capsule and was subsequently polymerized within the crack 
after contact with catalyst/initiator embedded in the matrix. 
[  11  ]  
 Recently, materials that use reversible dissociation of 
chemical bonds [  12  ,  13  ]  to repair macroscopic fractures, or reat-
tachments of amputated fragments, have been reported. The 
cross-linked copolymers are designed to retain their shape, 
and to improve the mechanical strength of the material, by 
incorporation of reversible chemical bonds that are utilized to 
reattach the fractured materials. Two approaches have been 
reported for such self-repair systems, one involving use of 
non-covalent 
[  14–17  ]  and the other reversible (dynamic) covalent 
bonds. [  18–27  ]  The advantages of the non-covalent bond approach 
include high reactivity and repeatability of the repair reaction. 
Macroscopic self-healing systems using hydrogen bonds, 
[  14  ]   π − π  
stacking [  15  ]  and metal-ion binding [  17  ]  have been examined. The 
second approach, based on reversible (dynamic) covalent bond 
formation 
[  12  ,  28–31  ]  could potentially provide higher mechanical 
strength and higher stability for the self-healing materials. Ini-
tially, systems based on reversible Diels-Alder reactions were 
examined, 
[  18  ,  19  ,  22  ]  followed by reversible reactions of imine 
moieties. [  20  ]  More recently, degenerative exchanges based upon 
bioinspired disulﬁ de exchanges [  24  ,  25  ,  32  ,  33  ]  and radical reshuf-
ﬂ ings of trithiocarbonates. [  21  ,  23  ]  
 These reversibly covalently cross-linked polymers have 
inherent low reactivities that require external stimulation; 
e.g., heat, UV, or the presence of low molecular weight chem-
ical reactants, often solvents, relatively long reaction times or 
an inert atmosphere to induce/allow repair. A recent trend in 
reversible covalent approaches is autonomous self-healing or 
weak stimulations. One of the key features to reach these goals 
is an appropriate choice of the covalent bonds, materials prop-
erties and reaction conditions. 
[  20  ,  26  ,  27  ]  
 Recently, we demonstrated the self-healing of covalently 
cross-linked polymers under light irradiation using reshufﬂ ing 
of trithiocarbonates. [  23  ]  These reactions required UV irradia-
tions, and had to be carried out in nitrogen atmosphere due to 
the carbon radicals as reactive intermediates. In this paper, we 
report macroscopic self-healing bulk system comprising cova-
lently cross-linked polymers based on reshufﬂ  ing of thiuram 
disulﬁ de (TDS) units, solely under the stimulation of visible 
light from a commercial conventional tabletop lamp in air, at 
room temperature and without solvent. Since the S-S bonds in 
the incorporated TDS units homolytically dissociate in visible 
light, the radical reshufﬂ ing reactions enable the reorganization 
of the linking units in the covalently cross-linked polymers 
[  34–37  ]  
and successful self-healing of signiﬁ cantly damaged polymers. 
 Various disulﬁ de compounds can degeneratively exchange 
in the presence of catalysts [  32  ,  38  ,  39  ]  or under UV irradiation [  40  ]  
but often require the presence of a solvent and an inert gas. 
This indicates that disulﬁ  de bonds with lower bond disso-
ciation energy and oxygen tolerance are necessary in order to 
prepare materials that can self-heal under milder conditions. 
Therefore, we examined use of a thiuram disulﬁ  de (TDS), a 
well known iniferter unit, 
[  41  ]  in order to test the efﬁ  ciency of 
degenerative exchange between TDS units in the presence of 
visible light. First, a model reaction using two low molecular 
weight compounds, TDS diol ( 1 ) 
[  42  ]  and tetraethyl TDS ( 2 ), was 
studied, as shown in  Figure    1  a. The reaction was carried out 
by mixing acetonitrile solutions of  1  and  2  at room tempera-
ture in the presence of air. Figure  1 b shows the variation in the 
molar fractions of the added symmetrical TDS molecules and 
the resulting mixed TDS product ( 3 ) with time, as evaluated by 
HPLC. With increasing reaction time, the molar fraction of  1  
and  2  in the reaction mixture gradually decreased from 0.5 to 
0.25, and that of  3  increased from 0 to 0.5. The molar ratio of 
reagents [ 1 ]:[ 2 ]:[ 3 ]  =  1:1:2 was reached already after 20 min, 
indicating that the complete scrambling of all three thiuram 
disulﬁ des reached the equilibrium state. In a dark place, how-
ever, the reshufﬂ ing reaction proceeded much slower, reaching 
the equilibrium state after 210 min (Supporting Information, 
Figure S1). This indicates that visible light triggered homolytic 
cleavage of the disulﬁ  de bond, as conﬁ  rmed by electron spin

 Reshufﬂ ing of TDS units could occur by two mechanisms, as 
shown in Figure  1 c. One is through a radical transfer reaction, 
upper scheme, in which the generated S-based radical reacts 
with a TDS unit to form a new TDS unit. Subsequently, the 
newly generated S-based radical reacts with another TDS unit 
in a cascade transfer process. The other possibility is a simple 
radical crossover reaction, Figure  1 c, lower scheme. It was 
previously reported that the S-based radicals generated from 
dithiocarbamate units are stable for more than two weeks. 
[  43  ]  In 
addition, it should be noted that this reaction proceeded under 
ambient visible light without any additional irradiation. 
 Precise polymer design, including structure of exchanging 
groups, chain mobility and surface-related properties, is impor-
tant for efﬁ cient self-healing of cross-linked polymers. In order 
to carry out the reactions at ambient temperature in the absence 
of a solvent, polymers with subambient  T  
g  should be selected. 
Also, chain mobility increases for shorter re-shufﬂ ing segments 
and for reactive units placed in the main chain separated from 
cross-linking points. 
[  23  ]  
 Therefore, in order to carry out the self-healing reaction in 
a bulk material at room temperature, the reactive TDS units, 
capable of re-shufﬂ ing, were incorporated in the main chain 
of a low  T  
g  polyurethane. The cross-linked polyurethane incor-
porating a TDS unit was synthesized by a simple polyaddi-
tion between a TDS diol ( 1 ), the reactive reshufﬂ ing unit, a 
tetra(ethylene glycol) (TEG) chain extender, a triethanolamine 
(TEA) cross-linker, and hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI) in 
DMF with dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL) as catalyst, as shown in

Increasing the molar ratio of TDS units in the network lowers

inferior. The original value was 74% and after 24 h exposure

cross-linking density of the matrix which reduces the mobility 
of the polymer chains, resulting in a more brittle material with 
reduced reactivity. 
 A deformation of cross-linked polymer with reversible cov-
alent bonds can be considerable, as conﬁ  rmed by dynamic 
mechanical analysis and creep measurements.

[  45  ]  The ability of the polymers to ﬂ ow under 
larger stress was evaluated by creep measurements, as shown 
that could be assigned to a S-based radical, [  44  ]  responsible for 
the self-healing process by reshufﬂ ing of TDS units. 
 The self-healing process requires exposure to visible light to 
generate S-based radicals. The healing process in the dark was 
less efﬁ cient (Figure S4, dark&dark). However, samples that 
had been previously exposed to light were successfully cured 
in the dark, indicating that the S-based radicals remain for a 
relatively long time and can reshufﬂ e on the macroscopic scale 
even without the continuous exposure to visible light (Figure S4, 
light&dark). 
 Nevertheless, the S-based radicals, although insensitive to 
oxygen, can participate in some side reactions. Surfaces cut 
and left separated for a longer time cannot heal as efﬁ  ciently 
as fragments placed in close contact after a shorter time period. 
The efﬁ ciency of self repair after extended periods of separation 
was studied by conducting tensile tests on samples with the two 
pieces left apart in air for 4 h or 24 h, before the self-healing 
reactions were carried out under visible light during 24 h. The 
results are shown in Figure S5. Increasing the time the sam-
ples were left apart in air from 4 to 24 h, resulted in lower 
elongation values, indicating a less efﬁ  cient self-healing. This 
could result from migration of the TDS radicals to the interior 
of the cross-linked polymer by continuous reorganization of the 
surfaces or through loss of CS 
2 , reducing concentration of the 
dithiocarbamyl radicals at the surface.

 In summary, we report the self-healing of covalently cross-
linked polyurethane, using radical reshufﬂ  ing of thiuram 
disulﬁ de units under ambient conditions; in the presence of 
visible light, in air, at room temperature and in the absence of 
a solvent. The visible light is available from natural sources and 
is less harmful than UV light. These systems could enable self-
healing of other covalently cross-linked polymers.
