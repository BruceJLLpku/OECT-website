Photoswitchable Dynamic Covalent Bonds

ABSTRACT: Combining dynamic covalent bonds with photoswitches
allows the kinetics and thermodynamics of exchange to be controlled with
light. However, this two-component strategy introduces synthetic and
compatibility challenges. Here, we present a single-component strategy
using donor−acceptor Stenhouse adducts (DASAs) as both the photo-
switch and the dynamic covalent bond. DASAs isomerize from “open” to
“closed” forms with light and heat, respectively. We discovered that open
DASA isomers undergo dynamic covalent exchange of their amine donor
via two pathways, reversible dissociation and conjugate transamination.
Exchange can then be arrested upon irradiation to form the closed DASA
isomer, offering a handle to gate dynamic behavior. Consequently,
incorporating DASAs as cross-linkers in PDMS-based networks yields covalent adaptable networks (CANs) with viscoelastic
behavior that can be tuned by light. We also identify degradation pathways that limit the reversibility of this system under extended
heating. Overall, DASA exchange represents a synthetically accessible platform for photocontrolled soft materials. More broadly, this
work introduces DASAs as a new class of intrinsically photoswitchable dynamic covalent bonds and lays the foundation for the
discovery of other stimuli-gated dynamic bonds that combine reactivity and responsiveness in a single molecular unit.
■
INTRODUCTION
Dynamic covalent chemistry has emerged as a powerful
strategy to control the assembly and reorganization of
molecules and materials using reactivity principles.
1
Among
the many applications of dynamic covalent bonds, covalent
adaptable networks (CANs) represent a promising opportunity
to combine the elasticity of permanent networks with the
ability to flow under conditions that permit bond exchange.
2−4
Tuning the kinetics and thermodynamics of the dynamic
bonds within the CAN in turn tunes key material properties
such as relaxation time, cross-link density, and degradability.
5,6
We can gain further spatiotemporal control over material
properties when we couple exchange to an external stimulus.
Among such stimuli, light is especially advantageous, providing
fast and precise localized control without direct physical
contact or additional reagents. Photocontrol is therefore
attractive for applications that demand on-demand reconfigur-
ability such as soft robotics,
7
microfabrication,
8,9
and adaptive
biomedical materials.
10,11
Reversible spatiotemporal control of dynamic covalent
bonds has previously been achieved using photoswitches,
chromophores that undergo rapid and reversible chemical
transformations upon light absorption. By engineering the
interaction of a photoswitch with one partner of a dynamic
covalent bond, thermodynamics and kinetics can be affected.
Design principles include (de)activating the dynamic bond
through valence bond tautomerization,
12−17
gating the activity
of an internal catalyst,
18,19
and preferentially stabilizing the
bound or unbound state (Figure 1a).
18,20,21
While this
approach is effective, it requires the incorporation of a separate
light-responsive unit in addition to the exchangeable bond,
demanding significant synthetic effort and thus hindering
scalability and iterative optimization. Compatibility between
the dynamic bond and the photoswitch is also a limiting factor.
For example, strong nucleophiles commonly used in dynamic
covalent bonds, such as thiols and amines, can compromise
photoswitch stability.
22−24
To overcome these limitations, we
envision that dynamic control could be achieved using a single
chemical entity capable of both bond exchange and photo-
switching (Figure 1b). This approach is underrepresented in
the literature
25−29
and has not been explored in CANs.
To achieve the goal of intrinsically photoswitchable dynamic
covalent chemistry, we focused on donor−acceptor Stenhouse
adducts (DASAs). Upon visible-light irradiation, the thermally
stable, highly colored “open” DASA isomer converts to a
colorless “closed” isomer.
30−32
This transformation reduces
optical density and enhances light penetration into the bulk
material, an advantage over other photoswitch classes.
33

In this work, we study the dynamic chemistry of the open

Small-molecule model systems reveal that the open DASA
isomer undergoes thermally activated dissociation into its
starting materials via the retro-aza-Piancatelli reaction, a
process that is not possible in the closed state. In addition to
this mechanism, we describe an alternative conjugate
addition−elimination pathway for transamination of the open

method for incorporating DASAs as cross-linkers in poly-
dimethylsiloxane (PDMS) CANs. Shear rheology measure-

behavior to the material, with a characteristic Arrhenius
dependence of relaxation time with temperature. Finally, we
show that DASA photoisomerization enables modulation of
network viscoelasticity, as evidenced by a reduction in creep
rate under continuous irradiation. Acting as a kinetic sink in
the dynamic covalent system, photoisomerization effectively
controls the material’s dynamic behavior. However, full

DASA Dynamic Covalent Exchange. We next sought to
confirm whether DASAs can undergo exchange via this
dissociative mechanism. We performed a crossover experiment
between mass-labeled DASA 5o and 6o at 100 °C. LCMS
analysis revealed partial scrambling of the amines, providing
direct support for dynamic exchange through this dissociative
mechanism (Figure 5). However, this reaction exhibited a
considerable induction period before the expected exchange
products were observed, with side reactions such as isomer-
ization and decomposition to 5−6d/d′ outcompeting
exchange (Figure S9). The induction period is the expected
result of a slow dissociation process producing low
concentrations of exchangeable partners and, consequently, a
slow rate for bimolecular reassociation.
Given recent literature on related polyenes, such as
spiropyran and cyanines,
28,53,54
we hypothesize DASAs could
also undergo an associative (bimolecular) transamination
exchange mechanism via addition of amine followed by
elimination (Figure 6a). To test the relevance of this
mechanism, we studied the reactivity of the DASA 5o in the
presence of an equimolar amount of amine 3 at 100 °C (Figure
6b). Under these conditions, rapid formation of the expected
exchange product 1o was confirmed. However, rapid and
irreversible isomerization of both DASAs to the β-enaminone
analogs, 1e and 5e, were also observed. The corresponding
closed isomers, 1c and 5c, were also detected as intermediates
in the amine-mediated isomerization of DASAs.
37,59
Once the
concentrations of olefin-migrated closed isomers 1e and 5e
reach a steady state, no further exchange is observed,
demonstrating that the β-enaminone does not exchange. If
exchange of these species were possible, we would expect 1e to
be favored over 5e by ∼8:1 based on the previously
determined equilibrium constants for DASA formation and
greater nucleophilicity of amine 3. The steady-state composi-
tion instead favors 5e by 1:7, consistent with these isomers
being kinetically trapped. Although thermal decomposition
products 1d and 5d were detected, this process was not found
to be significant under these conditions (<2%). Further studies
are required to understand why this decomposition pathway is
suppressed in the presence of added amine.
The role of basic additives in the system was analyzed by
performing exchange of 1o with 3 in the presence of DIPEA
(Figure S19). The addition of base resulted in an overall slower
exchange process. Interestingly, thermal decomposition to 1d
and 1d′ increased under these conditions. This latter
observation lends additional support for the proposed
hydrolysis mechanism for their formation (Figure S8)).
Initial rate kinetics experiments show that the rate of
formation of exchange products depends on the concentration
of the exchangeable amine (Figure S20). Power-law fitting of
the data provided a fractional rate order of ∼ 0.7, indicating the
free amines are partially involved in the rate-determining step.

DASA-Cross-Linked Covalent Adaptable Networks.
Given the tunability of DASA exchange observed in small-
molecule model systems, we sought to integrate DASAs as
dynamic cross-links into polymeric networks and investigate
their macroscopic viscoelastic behavior. Although a large body
of literature describes polymers with pendent DASAs, their
incorporation as cross-linkers remains unexplored.
33
Building
on protocols for installing pendent DASAs, we incorporated
DASA cross-linkers into an elastomeric network by cross-
linking a secondary amine-containing PDMS prepolymer with
a bifunctional activated furan (Figure 7a).
To simplify the material’s rheological behavior, we initially
selected commercial PDMS (M
n
12 kg/mol) below entangle-
ment molecular weight.
62,63
Both prepolymer P1 (10%
secondary amine functionalization, 10 amines/chain on
average) and the activated furan cross-linker 8 were readily
prepared in two steps from commercially available starting
materials at the multigram scale. Full synthetic details can be
found in the SI. Upon mixing P1 and 8 in CHCl
3
, a gel (Net-
1) with the characteristic dark purple color of the DASA was
formed. A second network (Net-2) using higher-molecular-
weight PDMS (P2, M
n
50 kg/mol) was also prepared in one
step from commercially available precursors. A lower
secondary amine content (3%) was used in Net-2 to maintain
approximately 30 amines per chain on average. To lower the
grafting density of the commercial PDMS prepolymers and
form materials with suitable properties for mechanical testing,
while avoiding side reactions of the excess secondary amines,
we alkylated a fraction of the secondary amines, forming
tertiary amines that do not undergo cross-linking.
Network formation was characterized using oscillatory shear
rheology, showing a gel point for the P1/8 mixture at 1800 s
(Figure 7b). The storage modulus continued to increase after
this time due to a combination of additional cross-linking and
solvent evaporation, approaching a maximum after 20 h. Based
on these results, dry polymer networks were prepared by
solvent casting P1 or P2 and 8 in a 1:1 ratio of donor and
acceptor, followed by curing at room temperature for 48 h to
allow slow solvent evaporation. The samples were then
annealed into flat films of uniform width by heating at 100
°C for 10 min under ten tons of ram force. Our small-molecule
kinetics studies suggest that <5% isomerization and decom-
position occur within 10 min. Control static networks with
amide cross-linkers, Control-1 and Control-2, were also
prepared using P2 and P2 according to literature protocols.

Stress Relaxation Measurements. The effect of DASA
cross-linkers on the strain response of the polymer networks
was evaluated using shear stress relaxation (Figure 8). These
measurements were performed in the linear viscoelastic regime
as confirmed by frequency and amplitude sweeps (Figures
S32−S33). As shown in Figure 8a, Net-1 exhibited substantial
stress relaxation over the measured time window compared to
the amide-cross-linked Control-1. The stress relaxation data
were fit to a stretched exponential model
72
to obtain a
characteristic relaxation time τ = 150 min at 100 °C. Due to
backbone entanglement, stress relaxation time of Net-2 was
slightly slower (τ = 200 min at 100 °C). The fitting factor β for
Net-1 and Net-2 were 0.47 and 0.44, respectively, consistent
with inhomogeneous materials that cannot be accurately
represented with a single Maxwell element. In contrast, the
control networks exhibited slow relaxation, retaining >75% of
their stress after 150 min. The stress relaxation data for
Control-1 and Control-2 were fit to the same stretched
exponential model to obtain extrapolated relaxation times of
500 and 1650 min at this temperature. This slow partial
relaxation is ascribed to chain mobility through defects like
dangling ends or background siloxane exchange; we note,
however, that the latter process generally requires a strong acid
or base catalyst.

Photoisomerization of DASA-Cross-Linked Materials.
To explore the potential for external control over the
viscoelastic properties of the network, we investigated the
photoisomerization behavior of DASA-based CANs. Although
there is a large body of evidence showing the ability of DASAs
to isomerize in solid matrices,
33
most reports incorporate them
only as pendent groups with high conformational freedom. We
were initially concerned that increased conformational
constraints imposed on DASAs as cross-linkers might impede
their photochromic behavior. To study photochromism within
the network, we dropcast Net-1 on a glass slide to make thin
films of the material (Section IIf). Despite being covalently
integrated as cross-linkers, we showed that DASAs retained
their ability to undergo reversible photoisomerization under
visible light irradiation (Figure 9a). Thermal back isomer-
ization was then promoted by gently heating the sample for 2
min at 80 °C, above its second thermal transition. Without
heating, isomerization back to the open form is not evident,
even after several days at room temperature. The absence of
reversion at room temperature is likely due to restricted
chromophore mobility within the high-T
g
, DASA-rich
domains.
UV−vis spectrophotometry was then used to quantify the
reversibility of the process by monitoring the relative
absorbance at 530 nm after several cycles of irradiation and
heating (Figure 9b). To minimize irreversible bleaching/
decomposition during extended irradiation or heating, which
are known phenonena,
33
we did not attempt to reach the
photostationary state during each cycle. As expected,
irradiation and heating of the samples results in partially
reversible changes in light absorption. The changes in
absorbance suggest that approximately 45 ± 9% of cross-
links undergo transformation per irradiation cycle, but this
value cannot be solely attributed to photoisomerization. In
addition to the desired photoisomerization, the incomplete
reversion indicates that irreversible photoconversion and
decomposition also contribute significantly. Some degree of
irreversible bleaching is likely caused by the harsh irradiation
conditions (100 mW/cm
2
). Nonetheless, FTIR measurements
on this material suggest a similar chemical composition is
recovered after the heating cycles, albeit without fully returning
to the initial state (Figure S38).
The network incorporates a significant fraction of free
tertiary amines as side-chains that accelerate irreversible
thermal isomerization to the colorless β-enaminone isomer,
as supported by our small-molecule model studies. These
results may suggest that the efficiency and reversibility of
DASA photoisomerization in networks could be further
improved through side-chain engineering or by tuning the
polymer matrix properties.

viscoelastic material such as a CAN continues deforming as
bond exchange reconfigures the network topology. To enable
the dynamic chemistry, these experiments were performed
above the upper thermal transition temperature. To avoid
rapid thermal back isomerization and decomposition, we
sought to avoid elevated temperatures, which rendered Net-1
unsuitable for these studies, as its upper thermal transition is

viscoelasticity corresponds to changes in the DASA-cross-link

open state to a static closed state. Irradiation favors the closed
form, which cannot exchange. In the dark, the open form is
able to exchange and rearrange network topology. Interest-

suggesting that cross-link density remains constant even as the

minimal dissociation, or that associative (conjugate addition−
elimination) exchange dominates at this temperature.

cannot exchange under these conditions. Altogether, these
results highlight the potential for light-responsive control over
the viscoelastic behavior of DASA-cross-linked CANs without
significantly altering network connectivity.

differences in reactivity between closed and open isomers,

dynamic cross-linkers into PDMS-based networks, forming
CANs with photoresponsive viscoelastic properties. Stress
relaxation confirmed that DASA cross-linkers impart viscoe-
lastic flow to the material, with relaxation behavior following an
Arrhenius temperature dependence. Significantly, we show
how DASA photoisomerization to the closed form leads to
deactivation of exchange and thus slows creep under
irradiation. This externally controlled viscoelasticity has the
potential for applications such as microfabrication and soft
robotics taking advantage of this viscoelastic−elastic tran-

prevent reversibility in this photoresponse for more than one
cycle of irradiation. While our results demonstrate exchange of
a first-generation DASA under nonpolar conditions, the
general applicability of DASA-based exchange across different
chemical environments, structures, and material platforms
remains to be established, and ongoing work is focused on
understanding these parameters and suppressing decomposi-
tion. We anticipate that this proof-of-principle study will lay
the groundwork for future exploration of this untapped
chemical space with transformative potential in the field of
light-controlled soft materials.
